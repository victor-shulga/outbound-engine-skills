# Named cold-email frameworks

Thirteen structures that practitioners have published under their own names, summarised in a few
lines each so you can pick one and brief a writer. The idea belongs to the person named; the wording
here is a summary, not their text. The original examples are not reproduced; read the creators' own
posts for those.

Two rules apply on top of every framework below:
- **CTA**: interest-based only (an interest question, an offer to send something, a routing question).
  No call, meeting or time-slot ask in a cold touch. If installed, `cta-interest-based` has the bank.
- **Proof**: any number, client name or result must be real and checkable. Placeholders stay
  placeholders until the user supplies the fact.

The collection was assembled in ColdIQ's public GTM skills; the attributions below follow it.

## Quick pick

| Situation | Framework |
|---|---|
| You can price the problem | Do the Math |
| A fresh trigger and little else | Short Trigger |
| The whole sector shares one known pain | Challenge of Similar Companies |
| You have a good third-party article or report | Neutral Insight |
| Senior buyer, curiosity about how they run something | Leader Responsibilities |
| You want to break the cold-email pattern | Ask Before Pitch |
| Your clients look like the prospect | Not Too Different Persona |
| You can hand over something useful today | Upfront Value |
| You have a lead magnet | Leverage Content |
| The prospect overpays or overworks for something simple | Why Are You Paying |
| You need clarity before writing anything | Write a Good Cold Email (problem-first) |
| You want it to read like a one-to-one note | Manual Email Style |
| You found a concrete flaw you can show | HOT Outreach |

## Do the Math (Thibaut Souyris)

- **Shape**: trigger (ideally with a number) → what you change → a back-of-napkin calculation of what
  that is worth for them → interest question.
- **Use when**: the problem has a cost the prospect already tracks (rework hours, missed bids, churn).
- **Watch out**: every input in the calculation must be either theirs (public) or labelled as a typical
  range. One invented figure sinks the whole email.

## Short Trigger (Guillermo Blanco)

- **Shape**: one personal trigger → one line that validates it and states the value → interest
  question. Under about 30 words.
- **Use when**: the trigger is specific and recent, and the value is easy to grasp.
- **Watch out**: with so few words, a vague trigger leaves nothing; skip this format if the trigger is
  generic.

## Challenge of Similar Companies (Patrick Trümpi)

- **Shape**: optional personal line → the challenge peers in their sector face → how you handle it →
  interest question → a P.S. that is personal or light.
- **Use when**: the pain is industry-wide and the prospect will recognise it without proof of their
  own case.
- **Watch out**: sector statistics need a source; "companies like yours" needs real companies behind it.

## Neutral Insight (Chelsea Castle)

- **Shape**: point to a credible third-party piece → why it matters for them now → why you are
  sharing it → optional soft nod to an earlier note.
- **Use when**: you have a genuinely useful article, report or talk that is not yours.
- **Watch out**: the resource has to stand on its own; a disguised sales page breaks the trust the
  format relies on.

## Leader Responsibilities (Vin Matano)

- **Shape**: address them by their role and ask, out of curiosity, how they handle one duty that role
  owns → one line on what you could offer around that duty → close with a genuine personal note that
  stands whatever their answer.
- **Use when**: senior buyers who respond to being asked about their craft.
- **Watch out**: the responsibility must be something that role really owns; a wrong guess reads as
  lazy research.

## Ask Before Pitch / Pattern Interrupt (Will Allred)

- **Shape**: open with the question you would normally end on → the observation that prompted it → the
  likely problem behind it → how you help → a P.S. on why it is relevant now.
- **Use when**: the audience is tired of standard cold emails and the observation is strong.
- **Watch out**: the opening question must make sense before the context; test that it does.

## Not Too Different Persona (BowTiedSalesGuy)

- **Shape**: name the group of people very like the prospect that you already work with → the
  specific problem that group keeps raising with you → interest question.
- **Use when**: you truly serve the prospect's peer group and know their language.
- **Watch out**: only claim the peer group you actually work with.

## Upfront Value (Jordan Crawford)

- **Shape**: deliver the useful thing in the email itself (a list, a finding, a short audit) and close
  with a line hoping it helps. No pitch.
- **Use when**: your data or research can produce something the prospect would pay a junior to build.
- **Watch out**: the value must be specific to them; a generic resource is just spam with manners.

## Leverage Content in Outbound (Ethan Parker)

- **Shape**: name the piece of content and what it helps with → "can I send it over?" → a P.S. on why
  it fits them now.
- **Use when**: you have a lead magnet that answers a question the persona already has.
- **Watch out**: the P.S. reason is what earns the reply; without a real trigger the email is a
  newsletter ad.

## Why Are You Paying? (Leif Bisping)

- **Shape**: a relatable everyday situation → the obvious choice in that situation → the parallel
  choice the prospect is making in their business.
- **Use when**: the prospect overpays or overworks for something that should be simple.
- **Watch out**: the analogy must be instantly clear; if it needs explaining, drop it.

## Write a Good Cold Email (Josh Braun)

- **Shape**: a thinking checklist before any writing: who exactly, which problem, how they solve it
  today, what is wrong with that way (and with the other options), how yours is different.
- **Rules**: describe what hurts about their current way of working before any value proposition;
  ban empty verbs such as "optimize", "streamline", "save time", "save money"; be specific.
- **Use when**: always, as the brief that comes before choosing any other framework.

## Manual Email Style (Josh Braun)

- **Shape**: reads like a one-to-one note: a real reason you noticed them, a modest claim of expertise,
  a line that lowers their risk, a curious (not pushy) interest question, an optional P.S. with
  plausible proof.
- **Idea**: lowering the prospect's "zone of resistance" (Braun's term) matters more than a strong
  claim; phrases like "not sure it's a fit" invite an honest answer.
- **Watch out**: the casual tone has to match the sender; a corporate brand writing like a freelancer
  sounds fake.

## HOT Outreach (creator not named in the source collection)

- **Shape**: three parts. Peak interest: the problem you found, or something non-obvious you noticed,
  or a real connection. The value: give part of the answer now, the thing they would otherwise pay
  you for. About you: one line on who you are and what you have done, then a question about their
  situation.
- **Use when**: you can show a concrete finding about their site, process or numbers.
- **Watch out**: end with a question about them; "let me know" and call asks are out.

## Building blocks that fit any framework

**First-line types** (from ColdIQ's playbook, 250K+ emails):
- *Observation*: something you noticed about them, stated without a reason. Works for general
  triggers because it leaves them curious.
- *Pain*: link a specific trigger to the problem it implies (hiring for a role → the gap that role is
  meant to fill). Stating the trigger alone ("noticed you're hiring") is weaker.
- *Industry*: a regulation, market shift or event the whole sector is dealing with. Fits enterprise and
  traditional industries.

Subject and preview work as one thought: the first ~50 characters of the body show in the inbox, so
the first line is the preview.

**Value-proposition styles** (same source):
- *Cost of the problem*: observation → what it is costing → a specific range.
- *Peer proof*: observation → what companies like them are doing → the result they get.
- *Specific outcome*: observation → one concrete outcome you produced → would it apply to them.

**Three-email arc** (Growth Engine X lessons, as collected by ColdIQ):
1. Why them, why now; the offer in a sentence; proof that others already use it; interest question.
2. Same thread, the context that did not fit in email 1.
3. Lower the ask: a resource, a short custom video or audit, a routing question.
Gaps of 3-5 days. Shift the value angle between emails (cost, revenue, time) and pass each claim
through a "so what" test: the benefit must end in something the reader cares about.

**Trigger patterns for a first line**: a relevant case for a similar company; an observation about
their site or offer; a role they are hiring for; headcount growing in a department; a tool in their
stack; ads they run; a known problem in their sector phrased as a question; an idea for a problem you
can see; and, for the last touch, a timing check.

**Breakup**: skip guilt and jokes about being ignored. Ask whether you have the right person, or who
else owns the topic.
